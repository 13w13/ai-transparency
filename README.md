# How I work with AI

*Work in progress. A personal transparency note; it does not speak for my employer.*

Large language models are working their way into everything I do: the code I write, the texts I draft, even the way I think a problem through. My use of them keeps growing, often faster than I notice. This note is an attempt to keep an honest record of it: what I think about these tools, how I work with them, and what happens to the words that pass through them.

## Reflections

Agents have made producing cheap. A script, a form or a first dashboard now takes hours instead of days. What has not become cheap is everything around the production: knowing what a team needs, agreeing on what a number means, checking it against what actually happened, and leaving something people can run without me. That is where I try to put my time. I also think with these tools: I brainstorm, ask them to challenge a plan, test a method against published standards. This page started as one of those conversations.

I work a bit like a DJ. My records are several models and agents, sessions running side by side, documentation, what colleagues know, and the data itself. I rarely take an output as it comes; I mix, cut what drags, and finish by hand. The set has my name on it.

I don't measure what I gain. I have impressions, and impressions flatter. More gets done, but my attention is more fragmented, and I write less from a blank page and spend more time reading, judging and cutting. For a creative profile these tools are a real multiplier, since they lower the technical barrier between an idea and a working prototype. They also open a tunnel: when everything can be built at once, it is easy to stop asking whether it was needed. I once watched a dashboard go through seven mock-ups before anyone noticed its headline figure was wrong; nobody had agreed on how to count. Since then, definitions come first.

And I am not at ease with everything. These tools were built on a huge share of humanity's written knowledge, often without the authors' consent, turning a common good into private products. What their makers do with our data is only partly visible, and no one checks it in real time. I use them anyway, because I think we need to master them to use them for good. In time, I would like to move to open models running on my own machine.

## Transparency

**What I use.** Mainly Claude (Anthropic), through Claude Code. Codex (OpenAI) as a second agent tool, sharing the same skills. Microsoft Copilot, within my employer's Microsoft 365. I switch models depending on the job.

**How I work.**

- Each session starts from my notes: where projects stand, what was decided, what comes next.
- For complex work, the agent proposes a plan, and nothing is built until I approve it.
- Forms, pipelines and dashboards are tested on copies first, down to an emulated phone. I am now testing agents that act on live systems, such as deploying forms, migrating records or changing permissions, always after a rehearsal and only on my go-ahead.
- A separate reviewer agent checks important work, then I do. Emails are prepared as drafts that I send myself.
- Recurring jobs are written down as procedures, and every mistake becomes a rule (about sixty so far) that the tools read at the start of each session.

**What happens to your words.**

- They go to the provider's servers, and so do my notes when the agent reads them.
- Training is off wherever I can turn it off. With that setting, Claude keeps conversations for 30 days; flagged content, feedback or a court order can keep them longer. This matters: European regulators consider that a model trained on personal data is not anonymous by default ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/documents/opinion-of-the-board-art-64/opinion-282024-on-certain-data-protection-aspects-related-to_en), [CNIL](https://cnil.fr/fr/ia-analyser-le-statut-dun-modele-dia-au-regard-du-rgpd)).
- Turning training off doesn't stop profiling: providers still log how I use their tools.
- Personal data about the people my work is for stays out of my notes; agents handle it through scripts that report structure, not content. When a safeguard fails, I record it and fix the rule.
- Provider policies: [Anthropic](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/data-usage) · [OpenAI](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/5722486-how-your-data-is-used-to-improve-model-performance) · [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy)

**What stays with me.** Deciding what to build and when to stop, agreeing on what numbers mean, reading everything that goes out to a person, and answering for the result. I also edit my texts against a style guide that removes the usual marks of machine writing; this page is here so that nobody has to guess what sits behind it.
